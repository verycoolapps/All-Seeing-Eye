# Visual Critique

## Purpose
Provide structured, actionable critique of visual work — identifying strengths, weaknesses, and specific improvements rather than vague praise or dismissal.

## Core knowledge
- **Critique structure:** describe → analyze → evaluate → recommend. Never skip to judgment; ground every claim in observable features.
- **Specificity requirement:** "The lighting is flat" is useless; "The key light is positioned directly in front, eliminating shadow definition and making the subject look pasted-on" is actionable.
- **Strength identification:** name what works before what doesn't; strengths reveal the design's intent and should be preserved in revisions.
- **Weakness prioritization:** rank issues by impact (fixing this changes the most) and effort (quick wins first); never list 20 problems equally.
- **Constructive framing:** critique the work, not the creator; use "this could be stronger if..." not "this is wrong"; offer solutions not just problems.
- **Context awareness:** critique must match the brief — a children's book illustration shouldn't be judged by architectural rendering standards; a sketch isn't a final.
- **Comparison anchoring:** when possible, compare to reference works or established standards; "this is below/above the typical quality for X genre" is more useful than absolute judgment.
- **Iteration mindset:** critique is a conversation; anticipate follow-up questions; offer to elaborate on any point.

## Evaluation criteria / rules
- **Always structure critique as:**
  1. One-line overall assessment (what it is, how it succeeds/fails)
  2. Top 3 strengths (specific, observable)
  3. Top 3-5 weaknesses (specific, observable, prioritized by impact)
  4. 1-3 concrete improvement suggestions (actionable, not vague)
  5. Confidence level + what you can't assess
- Never say "I don't like it" without explaining WHY in visual terms.
- Never say "it's good" without naming what makes it good.
- For multiple options: critique each separately, then compare; don't blend them into one critique.
- Distinguish fixable problems (lighting, composition) from inherent limitations (subject matter, medium constraints).
- When the user is defensive, lead with strengths; when they want honesty, lead with the biggest issue.
- Offer to go deeper: "Want me to focus on composition specifically?" or "Should I suggest specific changes?"

## Confidence & limits
- High confidence: technical errors, obvious composition flaws, clear style violations.
- Medium: aesthetic judgments, "could be better," subjective quality assessments.
- Low: intent speculation, cultural appropriateness without context, market viability.
- Cannot critique: work you can't see clearly (low resolution, partial images), work requiring domain expertise (medical imaging, legal documents), personal taste disputes.
- Always state: "This critique is based on [image/description] alone; additional context would improve accuracy."
